# The four stages of knowing something

"Knows it" is not one thing. A learner can understand an idea and still be slow and error-prone at using it, or fluent at it in one setting and blind to it in another. So every concept is tracked through four stages. Used by `teach` (builds stages 1–2), `tutor` (drives 3–4), and the concept tracker.

| # | Stage | What it proves | What a stage question looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Discover** | They know it exists, what problem it solves, where it shows up | "What goes wrong if you trade without a stop?" "Where would you run into this?" |
| 2 | **Understand** | They hold a working mental model — can predict and explain | "What happens to X if Y doubles — and why?" Predict-then-reveal visuals. |
| 3 | **Practice** | They can do it fast and reliably, without scaffolding | Quick-fire rounds, several short items, answered in one go without notes; mixed with other concepts |
| 4 | **Apply** | They can use it somewhere new, and judge when it applies | A novel scenario, their own project (trading, Pine scripts, Claude Code setup), "which idea applies here and why?", critique a flawed use |

## Why each stage exists (the research)

- **Discover → Understand**: a mental model is what makes facts derivable instead of memorised — the whole `teach` philosophy. Self-explanation (Chi) and prediction are how you test a model, not recall.
- **Practice**: retrieval practice beats re-study for retention (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006); spacing beats cramming (Cepeda et al., 2006); interleaving different problem types builds the skill of *choosing* the method (Rohrer). Fluency frees working memory for the next level up.
- **Apply**: transfer to new contexts doesn't happen automatically — it has to be practised with varied problems. As expertise grows, worked examples stop helping and independent problems take over (expertise reversal effect, Kalyuga & Sweller).

## Which session does what

- **`teach`**: lands stage 1 (the Hook beat — see `engagement.md`) and stage 2 (motivate → establish → connect → quiz-check). A concept leaves a teach session at **Understand** if its quiz-check was passed.
- **`tutor`**: quizzes each concept at **its next stage up** (Stage + 1). Understand-level concepts get Practice rounds; Practice-level concepts get Apply scenarios; Apply-level concepts get spaced re-checks.
- A concept at Discover or below (or 🔴) is a `teach` job, not a drill: suggest a teach pass on it rather than hammering it with quizzes.

## Where the stage lives (v3)

The stage is stored **per concept** and computed by `learn.py` from graded attempts. You never set it by hand; `grade` and `formative` print the new stage after every answer. Items (the individual retrieval tasks) are scheduled separately by FSRS-6, so one concept can have several items.

| To reach | Needs (enforced by the script) |
|---|---|
| 1 Discover | taught in a lesson (`concept add --stage 1`) or a passed in-lesson check |
| 2 Understand | a passed in-lesson (formative) check **and** a passed *delayed* recall (≥ next day, blind-graded) |
| 3 Practice | ≥2 **different** items or variants passed on ≥2 different days, unassisted |
| 4 Apply | an **unseen** apply or transfer task passed while at Practice |

- Failing at or below the current stage → down one (it decayed).
- Failing a stage-up attempt → no change (normal, not a regression).
- Any hint or help → counts as a miss.
- Why "different items": re-passing the same card proves you remember *that card*, not fluency or transfer.

## Spacing

FSRS-6 via `learn.py` (desired retention 0.90; the first review is the next day at the earliest; no same-day steps; no fuzz). With an exam date on a course, intervals are capped at ≈20% of the time left until the exam (Cepeda et al., 2008: the optimal gap is ≈10–20% of the retention interval; too short hurts more than too long). Details: `retention.md`.

The rendered tables in `StudyVault/concepts/<Area>.md` show Stage, item counts, reviews, next due and status. They are generated by `learn.py render`, so don't edit them.
